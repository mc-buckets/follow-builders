*The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck — "State of Enterprise AI 2026: Aaron Levie on Tokenmaxxing, Rise of Headless, and AI-Proofing Your Job"*

*The Takeaway:* AI's relentless pace of improvement is, paradoxically, the single biggest obstacle to enterprise adoption — because every breakthrough obsoletes the architecture companies just finished deploying.

Box CEO Aaron Levie has a front-row seat most people don't: he talks to hundreds of Global 2,000 CIOs every year while also being a power user who spends weekends shipping personal projects with AI agents. That dual perspective produces sharply contrarian reads on where enterprise AI actually stands.

The mood inside large enterprises is more optimistic than the cynical narrative suggests. CIOs are watching their engineering teams use Claude Code and Cursor and seeing real productivity gains — and now the business side is _pulling_ IT to bring those same gains to non-technical functions. But the rollout is genuinely hard, for a structural reason Levie articulates better than most: coding agents work so well partly because the codebase holds all the context, the user is technical enough to catch and fix agent mistakes, and the output is verifiable. Knowledge work has none of those properties. Context is scattered across 20 different systems (many not digital), access controls are a mess, and there's no clean way to QA the output.

On token costs — the surprise CFO line item of this year — Levie's take is that this isn't just subsidization unwinding. Frontier models are genuinely bigger and more capable, inference hardware isn't getting cheaper fast enough, and one coding agent can burn $1,000 of compute on a single task. AI spend is escaping IT budgets (typically 3–7% of revenue) and landing in line-of-business budgets — territory with no FinOps culture and no tooling to measure ROI on tokens. He floats the idea of a "$5 billion startup" just for AI compute ERP.

On headless software: it's additive, not a replacement. By database query volume, agents will dwarf human usage of enterprise software. But humans will still want graphical interfaces for complex workflows. Expect a seat model for end users *and* a consumption model for agents, running in parallel.

On jobs: Levie is a committed Jevons paradox believer. Most companies couldn't afford certain capabilities before, and now they can — which creates net new demand for humans to manage the agents doing that work.

_"The technology is getting so advanced that it makes obsolete the prior thing that you implemented, which actually means that the rollout takes longer — because we have no stable environment to roll things out in."_

The hottest new enterprise role: the internal FDE (field deployment engineer) — a technical person embedded in a business function whose job is to understand how people work and wire up agents to do that work safely, with the right data access and context.
