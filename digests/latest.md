*Claude Blog*

*<https://claude.com/blog/claude-for-foundation-models|Building intelligent apps for Apple platforms with Claude in the Foundation Models framework>*

Anthropic released a Swift package letting Apple developers use Apple's Foundation Models framework to call Claude for complex workflows. The pattern is a clean division of labor: Apple's on-device models handle fast, local tasks (summarization, extraction), while Claude takes over for multi-step reasoning, code generation, and web search. The two compose seamlessly — a journaling app can generate daily prompts on-device, then hand off to Claude to find thematic threads across months of entries, streaming everything back into the same SwiftUI view. Supported on iOS 27, iPadOS 27, macOS 27, visionOS 27, and watchOS 27. Requires an Anthropic API key.

*AI & I by Every — <https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLuMcoKK9mKgHtW_o9h5sGO2vXrffKHwJL|Best of the Pod: Wired's Kevin Kelly on Why AI Is a 50-year Overnight Success>*

_The Takeaway:_ Kevin Kelly argues we're as ignorant about intelligence today as Newton and his contemporaries were about electricity — and that should humble anyone making confident AGI predictions.

Wired co-founder and tech philosopher Kevin Kelly makes a striking historical parallel: Isaac Newton — one of the smartest humans ever — held fundamentally wrong beliefs about electricity. Early scientists theorized phlogiston, debated the ether, and could recognize electricity without understanding it. Kelly thinks we're in exactly the same position with intelligence. We can recognize it in each other, but we have no reliable theory of its components. His bet: intelligence is a _compound_, not an element — built from cognitive "elements" we haven't isolated yet, the way salt is a compound of elements nobody had named.

This framing has real consequences. If intelligence is a compound, then AI systems are like early chemistry: we're making useful things without understanding what they're made of, and we'll eventually fill out a rich taxonomy of minds. "I suspect that there is many compounds and that they will be engineered to do different things."

Kelly is candid about his prediction record: certain VR would explode after seeing it in Jared Lanier's lab in 1987 (it hasn't, yet), dismissive of eBay's potential, wrong about blockchain moving beyond finance. His takeaway: "It's easy to make predictions and hard to make predictions that are true." VR, he thinks, is still waiting for its LLM moment — a surprise breakthrough from an unexpected direction.

On AI as a creative tool: Kelly has been building elaborate AI-generated historical fantasy worlds (da Vinci, Martin Luther, and Columbus stranded in a hotel, eventually founding a science-and-religious-freedom city in the New World). The insight: the joy was entirely in the _making_, not the reading. He calls this the "audience of one" — a form of entertainment closer to Sunday painting than professional output, with no economic model except for the companies selling the compute.
