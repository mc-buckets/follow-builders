*No Priors — Powering AI-Driven Drug Discovery with President of Isomorphic Labs Max Jaderberg*

*The Takeaway:* Drug discovery has been failing not because of bad science, but because biology is a high-dimensional, combinatorially vast problem that empirical trial-and-error simply can't navigate at scale — and AI, specifically simulation models with experimental accuracy, may finally change the math.

Max Jaderberg is president and co-founder of Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet-backed company founded in 2021 by Demis Hassabis's team at DeepMind, with the stated mission of solving all disease. Jaderberg has a machine learning PhD focused on computer vision, sold a company to DeepMind in 2014, and worked on AlphaGo, AlphaStar, and the early AlphaFold results before helping spin out Isomorphic.

His central argument: the pharma industry has been stuck not because of regulatory overreach or bureaucracy, but because every drug is treated as a bespoke frontier science project. Without compounding learnings, there's no scale advantage. AI changes that by building a generalizable "drug design engine" — a platform that gets smarter with each drug it works on, compressing what used to take years of wet-lab iteration into in-silico simulation.

The most striking insight: rats are only about 30% predictive of human clinical toxicity. As Jaderberg puts it, if an AI scientist told him, "We've got this model, it's 30% predictive," he'd say "Maybe do a bit better than that." The goal is to replace animal testing with better in vitro endpoints, AI models, and new technologies like organoids and organs-on-a-chip to predict human outcomes before any drug enters a trial.

Isomorphic doesn't sell model access or tokens — they use their AI engine internally to discover drugs, both independently and in partnership with pharma companies. Current focus areas: oncology, immunology, and inflammation, with disease-agnostic models designed to work across modalities and targets.

"I'd be really disappointed if we fast forward even five years' time" without a step-change in predictive disease biology and molecule precision, Jaderberg said. The five-year benchmark he's watching: tools in place to start cracking large portions of disease in a fundamentally new way.
