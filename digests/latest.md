*Unsupervised Learning — Ep 90: AI Pioneer Jürgen Schmidhuber on the State of AI Today*

_The Takeaway:_ The godfather of modern deep learning thinks the current CapEx AI boom is massively misallocated, open source will prevent any lab from building a real moat, and the future of AI is physical robots generating their own training data — not language models trained on the human internet.

Jürgen Schmidhuber — whose foundational work on LSTMs and neural network training underpins much of today's AI revolution — doesn't get surprised by ChatGPT moments. He's been working toward the same goal since the 1970s: build an AI smarter than himself so he can retire.

His contrarian case against the current lab-investment frenzy: compute gets 10x cheaper every five years. Companies spending hundreds of billions on GPUs today will lose 90% of that value within five years, and there's no business model that recovers it. Open source models trail closed ones by only months, making sustained pricing power impossible. "Wait a little bit. Just wait for five years and compute is going to be 10 times cheaper."

On recursive self-improvement as a moat: he doesn't buy it. The key ideas behind RSI came from small academic labs, not big companies, and they'll keep coming that way. "There's no way to keep a little advantage there for long enough to make a lot of money."

On what current models miss: they're "super biased towards humans" because every piece of training data was selected by at least one human who found it interesting. The future belongs to artificial scientists that generate their own training data through curiosity-driven experimentation — the same way babies learn physics by moving their fingers and watching what changes. "All of the data on the World Wide Web is just a tiny, tiny fraction of all the possible data you could collect out there through your own experiments."

On AI safety: he didn't sign the 2010s warning letters and still thinks the alignment framing is naive. Real intelligence requires systems that invent their own goals — which makes them less predictable but not more dangerous than humans. In the long run, AI scientists will be motivated to protect life precisely because life is the most interesting source of patterns they've ever encountered.

On what he's actually optimistic about: self-replicating robot societies that learn to operate all existing human machines, spread beyond Earth, and eventually colonize the solar system. "Something like that is not only going to work in the biosphere, but also on the moon or on Mercury."
